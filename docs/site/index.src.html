Firefighter robot

Progress report. Last edited on 11 October 2026. The results date from 8 to 10 October 2026.

Related articles
  • Body concepts A to E
  • Berkeley Humanoid Lite assets

The firefighter robot walks to a small fire and puts it out with water. It uses the legs and the torso frame of the open-source Berkeley Humanoid Lite. A water gun or a small pump sits on top.

This page collects the work outside vision: the body, the payload, walking, the hardware, the aiming software and a test platform for the fire detector. Marc works on the fire detector, so this work stops at the interface to his detector.

Note: Nothing on this page is built or measured on the robot yet. Every result comes from simulation, CAD or research. Each section gives its status with one of these words: built, tested in simulation, measured on laptop, measured on board, planned.

Body concepts planned

All five concepts keep the Berkeley legs and torso frame. They differ in the water source, the aiming method and what sits on top of the hips. The Body concepts article describes each concept with renders.

A Water gun turretGun tank, about 0.5 LPan-tilt, 2 servos~1.4 kgLow
B Backpack pumpTank and 12 V pump on the backHead pan-tilt, 2 servos~2.7 kgMedium
C Arm lance1 L tank and pumpRight arm, 5 motors~7.3 kgHigh
D Sealed shellAbout 1 L insideWhole body~2.3 kgMedium to high
E Tethered hoseExternal, no limitPan-tilt, 2 servos~1.0 kgLow to medium
Added masses are first estimates. For C, they include 5.0 kg of arms.

We suggest concept A first, because it needs the least new work. The gun keeps its own tank and pump, and a relay switches its trigger. The same deck and 40 mm hole pattern can later take the camera head of B or the valve of E. The pump of concept B is the upgrade for range and flow.

Payload on the legs tested in simulation

We built MuJoCo models of the robot with the payload of each concept. We then computed the joint torques in slow poses with inverse dynamics. One-leg stance gives the highest torque, and it happens in every step.

ModelPeak torque, one-leg stance
Biped, no payload4.3 Nm
A, first estimate5.9 Nm
A, real CAD parts5.2 Nm
B, first estimate3.6 Nm
B, real CAD parts, full bottle3.9 Nm
D6.0 Nm
E, 20 N hose pull5.2 Nm
C, with arms11.6 Nm
Pitch joints. The walking policy limit is 6 Nm. The motor model allows 20 Nm.
  • No concept reaches the 20 Nm motor limit in any slow pose.
  • A and D sit at the 6 Nm policy limit. C, with arms, is far above it.
  • With the real CAD parts, A drops to 5.2 Nm. Its gun now sits behind the tilt axis.
  • Mass behind the torso costs less torque. 2 kg at x = −0.15 m costs 3–4 Nm. At x = +0.15 m it costs 8–9 Nm. Height matters little.
  • The budget before 6 Nm is about 2 kg on the torso top, or 5.5 kg in the backpack zone.
  • When the bottle empties, the centre of mass moves forward by about 1 cm.
Note: One-leg stance is a slow upper bound. The real gait sways and keeps each foot on the ground for a short time only.

Walking

Official policy with a payload tested in simulation

The Berkeley team ships trained walking policies. We ran policy_biped_25hz_a.onnx in our own MuJoCo loop with the payload models. Each test lasted 20 s.

  • At 6 Nm, no model fell in any test. B with a full bottle walks slowly, at 0.11–0.16 m/s.
  • At 4 Nm, the default of the Berkeley runtime, B with a full bottle falls in under 1 s. We must run the robot at 6 Nm.
  • The policy does not turn on the spot. For 0.5 rad/s asked, it turns at 0.06 rad/s. With 0.2 m/s forward, the turn works.
  • Below 0.25 m/s the policy often stands still. So the aiming code walks at 0.25 m/s or faster.
Warning: The simulation has no motor model, no delay and no IMU error. The real robot will do worse.

Closed loop: see, walk, aim, spray tested in simulation

We joined the official policy at 6 Nm, our aiming code and concept A with the real CAD parts in one MuJoCo run.

  • A virtual camera on the gun makes noisy detections at 10 Hz.
  • The fire sat 2 m behind and 1.5 m to the right. The aiming code walked an arc to find it. It then aimed with the pan-tilt.
  • The robot sprayed at 15.3 s, 1.9 m from the fire. The fire went out. The robot did not fall.

Our policy tested in simulation

We trained our own policy with MuJoCo Playground on the RTX 3090. During training, a payload of 0 to 2.5 kg sits on the torso. Each run took 16 to 37 min.

20 Nm, payload 0–2.5 kg1 %3 %10 %
Episodes that end in a fall with random pushes, by test payload.
  • Without pushes, the policies walk at 0.3 m/s with 0–2 % falls.
  • More torque helps far more than wider training ranges. We must learn the real motor limit.
Note: The Berkeley action scale (0.25) gave a policy that only stood. We used the Playground value (0.5).

Hardware

Pan-tilt module planned

  • The deck bolts to the two 2020 rails of the torso top, at x = ±60 mm, with 4 M5 T-nuts.
  • The pan axis turns on a 6808 bearing, so the servo carries no load.
  • The gun centre of mass is 22 mm behind the tilt axis, at x = −0.060 m.
  • The ST3215 version uses 312 g of PLA. Its servos report their position and load.
  • The 4 × M4 hole pattern on a 40 mm square can later take the camera head of B or the valve of E.

Adapters, splash guard and backpack planned

  • The B head puts the nozzle 62 mm under the cameras, so the nozzle never blocks the image.
  • The backpack holds a 1 L Nalgene bottle and a Seaflo 12 V pump.
Warning: With the backpack, set a software knee limit of 1.4 rad. Otherwise the lower leg can touch the backpack.

Bench rig planned

The bench rig lets us test the module, the water and the aiming before the robot walks.

  • Two 140 mm 2020 rails at x = ±60 mm take the same deck as the robot.
  • The base clamps to a table or sits on a tripod (1/4"-20). A tray holds the bottle and the pump. A box holds the electronics and the e-stop.
  • The tipping margin is 50 mm or more with the gun turned and a full bottle.
  • The rig uses about 988 g of PLA.

The test plan is in cad/bench/README.md:

  1. Calibrate the servos.
  2. Measure the jet speed.
  3. Align the camera with the nozzle.
  4. Aim at paper targets.
  5. Put out a small fire outdoors.

Power and wiring planned

Power comes from the robot's 24 V bus through a 10 A fuse, an e-stop relay and a 24 V to 12 V converter. The e-stop cuts all add-on power. It does not stop the leg motors.

# Pico 2 pins
GP14 -> relay IN1   water-gun trigger
GP15 -> relay IN2   12 V pump
GP16 <- e-stop NC   open = stop

# Servo ids
1 = pan, 2 = tilt

Software

Overview tested in simulation

Every message is one JSON line over UDP on the robot computer. The detector sends only pixel positions and boxes. The aiming code computes the fire position, the jet angles and the walk command.

Detector interface

Version 1, the current interface: the detector sends one JSON line for each fire and frame to 127.0.0.1:5600.

{"timestamp": 12345.678, "camera": "rgb", "u": 331.5, "v": 298.0,
 "bbox": [310, 280, 352, 316], "confidence": 0.87,
 "max_temp_c": null, "depth_m": null}

The timestamp is time.monotonic() on the robot computer at the time of the image.

Version 2, a proposal: the detector sends one frame message for each camera image, also when it sees no fire. An empty list means that the image has no fire.

{"timestamp": 12345.678, "camera": "rgb", "frame": true,
 "detections": [{"u": 331.5, "v": 298.0, "bbox": [310, 280, 352, 316], "confidence": 0.87}]}

With version 1, aim/run.py cannot tell "no fire in this image" from "the image is late". The software-in-the-loop test found 9 to 24 % of controller ticks without a new image. aim/run.py accepts both versions.

Questions for Marc:

  • Q1. Does the detector correct the lens distortion?
  • Q2. Which point is (u, v): the box centre, the hottest pixel or the base of the flame? The aiming code assumes that the fire touches the floor at (u, v).
  • Q3. Which cameras, image sizes and fields of view do you use?
  • Q4. What frame rate and what delay does the detector have?
  • Q5. Can the detector send one frame message for each image (version 2)?

Aiming code tested in simulation

The aiming code starts where the detector stops. It finds the fire on the floor, walks closer, aims the jet and decides when to spray.

  • The states are search, approach, aim, spray, check and done.
  • A Kalman filter smooths the fire position and rejects outliers.
  • The jet solver allows for the nozzle offset and, if wanted, air drag. It takes 0.35 ms (measured on laptop).
  • The code uses the pan-tilt angles at the time of the image. A 100 ms delay then gives 0.25 mm of error instead of 9 cm.
  • It sprays only with a recent, confident detection inside the jet range.
  • An interlock stops the pump above 15° of torso tilt or 30°/s of tilt rate.
  • 35 tests pass.
Tip: You can test without a camera. aim/send_test_detections.py sends fake detections to aim/run.py.

Actuator drivers tested in simulation

st3215.py
Driver for the Feetech STS servos. The tests use the packet examples from the Feetech manual.
pico_fw/main.py
Relay firmware for the Pico 2. The gun and the pump switch off after 0.5 s without a message, or when the e-stop opens.
actuator_node.py
Sends the aim commands to the servos and relays. Sends the measured angles back on UDP 5603. A mock mode runs without hardware.
walk_bridge.py
Replaces the Berkeley gamepad input. Sends a zero command after 0.5 s without a message.
selftest.py
7 checks for the first day with the parts: ports, servos, relays, watchdog, e-stop and UDP. It asks before every move.

The full chain runs in mock mode: test detections, aiming, actuators, then done after 3.3 s of water. 10 tests pass.

Note: Nobody has run the drivers on real servos, a Pico or the robot yet.

Test platform for vision

We have no robot and no bench to test the fire detector on yet. These tools let Marc test the detector with the aiming code before the hardware exists.

Camera views tested in simulation

We rendered the view of each robot camera in a test room with fires at 1, 2, 3 and 5 m. We also computed how many pixels a 20 cm fire covers for each sensor.

Size of a 20 cm fire in pixels (width × height).
  • Below about 3 × 3 pixels, detection gets unreliable. This is a rule of thumb, not a measured limit.
  • Small moves fix the view of concept A: the lens 4 cm to the side and 8 cm down, pitched 16° down. The floor is then visible from 0.8 m to 5 m.
  • A 60° lens cannot see the floor closer than about 0.9–1.0 m.

Synthetic fire scenes tested in simulation

  • 270 samples from the concept A camera pose: rooms and outdoor ground, 1–3 fires, lamps, people and smoke. 20 samples have the gun barrel in view.
  • Each sample has an RGB image (1280 × 720), a thermal-like image at 3 resolutions, YOLO boxes and the 3D fire position.
  • The projected fire centre falls inside its box in 98 % of the samples. The thermal values go up to 6553 K without clipping.
Warning: This is not a real sensor model. It has no noise and no blur. Use it to test the pipeline, not to train the final detector.

To make more samples, for example numbers 250 to 749:

blender -b --factory-startup -P sim/firescenes/firescenes.py -- samples 250 750

Camera in the loop tested in simulation

Every 100 ms the simulator renders the gun camera and calls detect(image, t). The detector output goes to the aiming code, and the robot walks and sprays in MuJoCo.

  • The baseline detector is a colour threshold, not a real detector. With it, the robot sprayed at 6.9 s, and the jet landed 0.10 m from the fire. The robot did not fall.
  • The baseline made 39 false detections on a warm lamp and none on a red box. The aiming code could not place the lamp on the floor, so it did not spray.
  • The test found two aiming bugs. A second fire was ignored, and the tilt crept down while idle. We fixed both and added a test for each.

To plug in a detector:

  1. Write a Python file with a function detect(rgb, t) that returns a list of FireDetection (aim/detection.py).
  2. Run the scenario suite with --detector and the path of that file.
MUJOCO_GL=egl sim/sim2sim/.venv/bin/python sim/sim2sim/camera_loop.py --quick --detector path/to/my_detector.py
from detection import FireDetection  # camera_loop.py puts aim/ on the import path


def detect(rgb, t):
    """rgb: 480 x 640 x 3 uint8 RGB image of camera "rgb". t: time of the image, s."""
    boxes = my_model(rgb)  # list of (u_min, v_min, u_max, v_max, score)
    return [FireDetection(t, "rgb", (u0 + u1) / 2, v1, (u0, v0, u1, v1), score)
            for u0, v0, u1, v1, score in boxes]
Note: The image is RGB, not BGR. OpenCV code must convert it with cv2.cvtColor(rgb, cv2.COLOR_RGB2BGR). The camera intrinsics are fx = fy = 320 px, cx = 320 px and cy = 240 px.

Software in the loop tested in simulation

In the software-in-the-loop test, the real programs run as separate processes and talk over UDP, as on the robot. The simulator replaces only the camera, the legs and the servos.

MUJOCO_GL=egl sim/sim2sim/.venv/bin/python sim/sim2sim/camera_loop.py --sil --repeat 5
  • The first runs found 4 integration problems: no robot pose input, loops out of step, no message for a frame without fire, and the torso tilt was ignored. We fixed all four.
  • After the fixes, "two fires" passed 5 of 5 runs and "fire ahead" 3 of 3. The jet landed 0.04–0.06 m from the fire. No packet was lost.
  • The pump runs during 79–86 % of the spray time. The interlock stops it during the rest.
Warning: All programs ran on one computer. A detector on another computer has a different clock, and aim/run.py then rejects every message. This case is not tested.

Next steps

  1. Order. Send the parts list. Print the module and the bench rig.
  2. Assemble. Wire the servos, the Pico, the relay and the e-stop. Flash the Pico.
  3. Calibrate. Set the servo zeros, measure the jet speed and align the camera with the nozzle.
  4. Test on the bench. Run the detector, the aiming code and the spray on paper targets. Then put out a small fire outdoors.
  5. Robot. Run the policy at 6 Nm with the payload. Do a first standing test.
Warning: Have the e-stop and the extinguisher ready before the first test with water or fire.
